# A six-week teen reading programme on a $500 budget

Chosen as the swarm's widest win. Problem `teen-reading-programme`.

## Problem

A public library branch in a mid-sized town wants to run a six-week programme for 13-to-17-year-olds, most of whom do not use the library today. One librarian can give four hours a week; volunteers can be recruited; the budget is $500 for everything beyond the room and the existing collection. The branch manager will repeat the programme only if there is evidence it changed something for the participants, not just that some turned up.

Design the programme: what happens each week and why teenagers who do not read for pleasure would come, how the library reaches teens who are not already visitors, how the $500 is spent, and how the outcome is measured within the six weeks in a way that goes beyond attendance. Make sure it fits four hours of librarian time a week.

### Constraints

- A public library branch in a mid-sized town; one librarian can give four hours a week, plus volunteers if recruited.
- Budget is $500 in total for the six weeks; the library already has a meeting room and a book collection.
- The target is 13-to-17-year-olds, most of whom do not currently use the library.
- The branch manager wants evidence of the outcome, not only attendance numbers, to decide whether to repeat it.

### Success criteria

- A programme design (format, frequency, content) that a non-reading teenager would plausibly turn up to.
- A recruitment plan that reaches teens who do not use the library today.
- A budget that allocates the $500 and shows what it buys.
- Outcome measures beyond attendance, measurable within six weeks, and how they are collected.

## Swarm answer

_Run `01a1c40a-f90f-7b03-9eaf-31ff9f281d43`, 12 rounds, completed/budget_rounds, $0.0280, confidence 0.40._

Recommendation: run a six-week Teen Maker Hub pilot, one 60-minute hands-on session a week for up to eight 13-to-17-year-olds, staffed by the librarian and two volunteers at a 1:8 ratio. Treat it as a conditional pilot: the board does not yet show that teens who do not read will attend to the end or that the programme changes anything, so the branch should go ahead only if the baseline and outcome measures below are in place before Week 1.

Why teens would come: the activities are things a non-reader can start on day one with no reading required, such as making a zine, upcycled jewellery, comic art, and a short audio story recorded on phones. Free food is the main attendance hook, because teens who are not current library users are mainly drawn by tangible incentives. Week 5 lets teens choose the project they finish, and Week 6 is a showcase where they display what they made, which gives the later weeks a concrete goal. Weekly content: Week 1 zine making; Week 2 upcycled jewellery; Week 3 comic art; Week 4 audio storytelling on phones (no library tablets or soundboards are assumed, since the board does not confirm they exist); Week 5 teen-chosen project; Week 6 showcase.

Librarian time: each week the librarian has four hours, split as about one hour of setup and cleanup, one hour of facilitation, one hour of admin, and one hour of recruitment and data collection. Two volunteers handle registration, snacks, and supervision support. Before Week 1, parental consent forms must be signed and volunteer background checks completed, and the 1:8 ratio must be kept in every session.

Recruitment: the branch should reach non-users through high school media clubs such as yearbook, drama and robotics, using flyers and a free taster session with pizza. Peer Captains from schools can distribute QR codes for a first-visit food voucher. The board does not show that these channels reach teens who do not already engage in creative activities, and the captains may mostly recruit already-active students. Digital ads are dropped, because the board shows teens learn of opportunities mainly through peers and family rather than online marketing.

Budget, totalling $500: about $250 for food vouchers, covering roughly six weeks of food for eight teens at the per-session cost in the board's cost analysis; about $150 for zine, jewellery and audio consumables, which is probably too low, since the board estimates consumables at about $10 per participant per week and the materials line has not been reconciled with that figure; $50 for printed flyers and posters for schools and community centres; and $50 contingency, which also covers thank-you food for Peer Captains. The board does not fund cash or gift-card incentives for Peer Captains, so these are replaced with food vouchers. If materials prove too costly, the programme should cut the number of craft items rather than the number of sessions.

Outcome measures beyond attendance: teens earn Skill Passport stamps for behaviours that show change, such as signing up for a library card, completing one zine or jewellery item, finishing an audio clip, and attending the showcase. Attendance alone must not earn enough stamps to reach the threshold, because attendance can count for four of the stamps and otherwise make the outcome look positive. The success threshold is more than 50 percent of participants earning three or more stamps including at least one made item, plus a positive shift in a three-question pre and post survey. The baseline survey is taken in Week 1 and the post survey in Week 6. Stamps and survey responses are collected from every teen who attends any session, not only those who reach the showcase, so that drop-outs are counted. Survey results are weak evidence because teenagers' self-reports are affected by social desirability and short-term engagement, so they should be read alongside the behavioural stamps. The showcase poll asking whether teens learned something new is only a secondary, low-weight measure.

Main caveats and open questions: there is no comparison group, so any change cannot be attributed to the programme alone. Retention beyond Week 3 is unproven. The materials budget may not cover the craft plan. Recruitment of genuine non-users is untested. The board has no evidence on whether the stamp threshold predicts real change in reading, library use or confidence. Before the branch decides to repeat the programme, it should check whether at least half of the teens who enrol reach Week 4, whether the materials cost stays within the line, and whether the stamps and survey show the pattern described above.

**Recommendation**

Run a weekly 90-minute after-school 'make-a-thing' studio, not a reading club. Teens build a zine and audio project about their own town, using phones, free software and the library's collection as raw material. Recruit through the places teens already are, using peer invitations and one partner organisation rather than library posters. Spend the $500 mainly on food, a small audio kit and printing. Judge success against targets set in advance: how many participants were genuine non-users, how many came back, whether they got and used a library card, whether they finished something, and whether their sense of the library changed. All of it runs on four librarian hours a week plus two or three volunteers, though recruitment before launch needs extra hours (see the caveats).
