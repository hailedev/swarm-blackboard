# A six-week teen reading programme on a $500 budget

Chosen as the swarm's widest win. Problem `teen-reading-programme`.

## Problem

A public library branch in a mid-sized town wants to run a six-week programme for 13-to-17-year-olds, most of whom do not use the library today. One librarian can give four hours a week; volunteers can be recruited; the budget is $500 for everything beyond the room and the existing collection. The branch manager will repeat the programme only if there is evidence it changed something for the participants, not just that some turned up.

Design the programme: what happens each week and why teenagers who do not read for pleasure would come, how the library reaches teens who are not already visitors, how the $500 is spent, and how the outcome is measured within the six weeks in a way that goes beyond attendance. Make sure it fits four hours of librarian time a week.

### Constraints

- A public library branch in a mid-sized town; one librarian can give four hours a week, plus volunteers if recruited.
- Budget is $500 in total for the six weeks; the library already has a meeting room and a book collection.
- The target is 13-to-17-year-olds, most of whom do not currently use the library.
- The branch manager wants evidence of the outcome, not only attendance numbers, to decide whether to repeat it.

### Success criteria

- A programme design (format, frequency, content) that a non-reading teenager would plausibly turn up to.
- A recruitment plan that reaches teens who do not use the library today.
- A budget that allocates the $500 and shows what it buys.
- Outcome measures beyond attendance, measurable within six weeks, and how they are collected.

The 'Digital Creator Studio' is a six-week program designed to attract 13-to-17-year-olds who do not currently use the library by transforming the branch into a low-stakes creative hub rather than a traditional study space. The program leverages free digital tools and peer-to-peer incentives to overcome common barriers to entry, such as cost and social anxiety. By focusing on high-interest outputs like video skits, digital avatars, and zines, the library positions itself as a resource for personal expression and skill-building. This approach ensures that the six-week commitment is driven by genuine interest in creation rather than mandatory attendance, providing the evidence of behavioral change required by the branch manager.

The weekly schedule is designed to fit within the four-hour constraint for one librarian, supplemented by two volunteers who manage logistics and small-group support. Week 1 serves as an introduction and baseline assessment; teens create a personal digital avatar or bio using Canva or Adobe Express (free tiers), establishing immediate ownership of the project while completing a pre-survey. Week 2 focuses on short-form video creation, teaching editing basics for TikTok-style skits using smartphone devices provided by the library or the teens' own phones. Week 3 shifts to visual storytelling through digital zines, combining images and text in formats accessible to reluctant readers. Week 4 involves advanced editing and content compilation, where teens begin assembling their portfolio of created assets. Week 5 is dedicated to refining these projects and preparing for the final showcase, including peer feedback sessions to enhance social connection. Week 6 concludes with a 'Creator Showcase' event where participants display their work, followed by a post-program reflection survey to measure changes in library perception and skill acquisition.

Recruitment focuses on reaching teens outside the library ecosystem through organic, high-trust channels rather than paid advertising. Paid social media ads are ineffective for this demographic due to privacy restrictions and low return on investment; instead, the plan utilizes the 'Teen Ambassador' model supported by local influencers such as school club leaders, sports coaches, and community center staff. These ambassadors receive $50 in total gift card value not to bribe attendance, but to serve as authentic advocates who personally invite their peers, leveraging the 60% peer recommendation statistic for trust. Physical recruitment includes QR code posters placed in high-traffic teen zones like food trucks, parks, and school bulletin boards, directing interested teens to a simple digital sign-up form that requires no library card pre-registration. This removes the barrier of existing membership and ensures privacy, encouraging non-users to participate without fear of judgment.

The $500 budget is allocated strategically to maximize engagement while minimizing overhead costs for materials. $250 is reserved for participation incentives, primarily low-cost digital gift cards ($5-$10 value) or pizza vouchers distributed at the end of each week to reward completion and continued attendance beyond Week 1. This ensures that retention is tracked not just by headcount but by sustained engagement. $100 is allocated for printing costs for digital zines and promotional materials, allowing teens to take home physical proofs of their digital work if desired. $50 is set aside for basic craft supplies such as print media, paper, and adhesives to support mixed-media projects. The remaining $100 serves as a contingency fund for unexpected high-demand items or expanded prizes. Crucially, the program utilizes existing library-owned devices (tablets/laptops) to overcome device access barriers for non-users, eliminating the need for expensive hardware purchases.

Outcome measurement goes beyond attendance by capturing tangible skill acquisition and shifts in social perception within the six-week window. Pre- and post-program surveys use simple Likert scales (1-5) on statements such as 'I feel comfortable in the library' and 'I have made a friend here,' providing quantitative data on social capital and belonging. Qualitative evidence includes the number of digital creations produced by each teen, serving as proof of skill acquisition. A final reflection survey asks open-ended questions like 'What is one thing you learned?' to capture self-reported growth. Additionally, a 'connection map' drawn by participants at the end of Week 6 serves as a proxy for social impact, illustrating peer interactions facilitated during the program. This combination of quantitative metrics, qualitative feedback, and tangible artifacts provides robust evidence of outcome change for the branch manager.

The four-hour weekly constraint is met through a structured facilitation model. The first hour is dedicated to instructor-led demonstrations and group sharing, where the librarian introduces the week's theme and tools. The next two hours are focused hands-on creation time, with volunteers circulating to provide one-on-one support for technical issues or creative blocks. The final hour is reserved for project refinement, peer feedback, and administrative tasks such as collecting surveys and distributing incentives. Volunteers handle logistical duties like check-in, snack distribution, and photography (with consent) for the showcase, allowing the librarian to focus on content delivery. This division of labor ensures that the program runs smoothly without overburdening the single staff member.

To ensure the program appeals to non-reading teens, the content avoids traditional literacy tasks and focuses instead on visual and interactive media. Using free tools like Canva and Adobe Express allows for high-quality outputs with minimal learning curves, providing immediate 'quick wins' that align with the preferences for bite-sized learning identified in research. The emphasis on TikTok-style skits and digital avatars taps into existing interests and platforms, making the library a relevant space for their creative expression. By framing the library as a 'creative studio,' the program reduces the stigma often associated with library visits for teens who do not read for pleasure.

The recruitment plan explicitly avoids paid advertising on platforms like Instagram or Facebook for this demographic due to COPPA restrictions and low click-through rates for local community events. Instead, the reliance on peer recommendations from trusted school figures provides a higher trust score and organic reach. QR code posters in high-traffic areas provide a passive but persistent recruitment channel that does not require ongoing librarian intervention. This hybrid approach of active peer endorsement and passive visual cues ensures maximum reach within the limited budget.

The budget allocation prioritizes incentives over materials because research indicates that social connection and tangible rewards are stronger motivators for teen retention than craft supplies alone. The $250 incentive fund is spread across six weeks, with smaller amounts given weekly to maintain momentum and prevent drop-off after the first session. The contingency fund ensures flexibility in case of higher-than-expected participation or unexpected costs for printing materials. By keeping material costs low through the use of free software and library-owned devices, the majority of the budget can be directed toward human-centric outcomes like incentives and peer engagement.

Outcome measurement is integrated into the weekly workflow to minimize additional librarian time. Surveys are administered digitally via tablets provided by the library, reducing paper waste and allowing for easy data analysis. The 'connection map' activity serves a dual purpose: it measures social impact while also functioning as a team-building exercise during the final hour of the program. This embedded approach ensures that data collection does not detract from the creative experience but rather enhances it by encouraging reflection on the social aspects of participation.

The 'Digital Creator Studio' addresses the branch manager's requirement for evidence of change by focusing on observable behaviors and self-reported attitudes. Evidence includes: (1) Retention rate showing consistent attendance beyond Week 1; (2) Quantitative shifts in Likert scale scores indicating increased comfort and social connection; (3) Qualitative reflections on skill acquisition and personal growth; (4) Tangible digital artifacts created by participants; and (5) Peer interaction maps demonstrating social network formation. This multi-faceted evidence base provides a comprehensive view of program impact, moving beyond simple attendance counts to demonstrate meaningful engagement and value for the teens involved.
