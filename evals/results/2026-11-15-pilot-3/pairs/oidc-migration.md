# Moving a monolith from home-grown sessions to OIDC without downtime

Chosen as the swarm's widest win. Problem `oidc-migration`.

## Problem

A product company runs a monolith with its own login: usernames, bcrypt password hashes and server-side sessions, about 40,000 active users. It has bought a hosted identity provider that speaks OIDC and can migrate credentials through a hook at login time. Three engineers have one quarter, alongside their product work, to move authentication to the provider. There may be no planned downtime, no forced password reset, and no step that cannot be undone within an hour if it goes wrong.

Plan the migration: the ordered steps across the quarter and what is true after each, how existing users move over (including users who do not log in during the quarter), how sessions behave while both systems are live, and where the point of no return is and what gets cleaned up after it.

### Constraints

- One quarter, three engineers part-time alongside product work.
- About 40,000 active users with passwords stored as bcrypt hashes in the monolith's database; users must not be forced to reset passwords.
- The company has chosen a hosted identity provider that supports OIDC and can import or migrate credentials through a hook.
- No planned downtime and no "big bang" switch: a failed step must be reversible within an hour.

### Success criteria

- An ordered plan of steps across the quarter, each reversible, with what is true at the end of each.
- How existing users move without a forced password reset, and what happens to users who do not log in during the migration.
- How sessions behave during the transition when both systems are live.
- The point of no return, how it is reached safely, and what is cleaned up afterwards.

## Swarm answer

_Run `01a1e163-b288-7903-9abe-cd775f1dd76d`, 12 rounds, completed/budget_rounds, $0.0225, confidence 0.70._

Recommendation: migrate in phases behind per-user feature flags, with the monolith remaining the sole session authority throughout. The monolith keeps its bcrypt verifier as the source of truth until the point of no return (PoNR). Active users move through a login-triggered asynchronous import: at login the monolith verifies the bcrypt hash locally and, only on success, calls the provider's import hook, preserving the existing hash, cost and salt without re-hashing. No password reset is ever forced, and each step is reversible by flipping a flag within an hour.

Rollout order: provider setup and hook integration in shadow mode, then an internal pilot cohort, then a 5% beta cohort with monitoring, then full enablement of the login-triggered import for all active users. Migrated and unmigrated accounts are distinguished by per-user state flags. A failed or throttled hook call must not block login: the account is marked pending_retry, the monolith keeps verifying locally, and retries continue until success or a timeout of about 30 days.

Sessions: the monolith issues its own signed cookie after validating the OIDC ID token, so existing session handling and permission checks are unchanged. The local session maximum age is capped at the OIDC refresh token duration. Logout and revocation are handled by token introspection on sensitive actions and periodically, and by provider webhooks for suspension or deletion where supported, so no zombie session survives a provider-side revocation.

Dormant users (those who do not log in during the quarter) keep their original bcrypt hashes in the monolith database. After the PoNR, a background batch job imports their hashes through the provider's bulk import hook, which accepts raw bcrypt hashes within supported cost ranges. Any hash the provider rejects is logged for manual handling. Dormant users who later return are routed to OIDC via the login-triggered path, so no login path conflicts.

Point of no return: it is reached by a feature flag that disables new legacy password verification for logins, and only once more than 95% of active users are migrated and the provider has been stable for 24 hours. Legacy code and the hash column stay readable through a soak period of at least two weeks or until legacy sessions expire, whichever is longer, so the one-hour reversal remains available. Only after the soak are the legacy auth code removed and the hashes scrubbed.

Open items: a provider outage after the PoNR flag is flipped still needs a defined runbook; whether the provider's bulk import accepts the monolith's bcrypt cost factor at the scale of about 40,000 accounts must be verified before cutover; and the exact revocation coverage depends on which OIDC features the chosen provider supports. Confidence is held below 0.8 until these are confirmed.

## Baseline answer

_claude-sonnet-5-5, asked for about 1350 words, used 2689 of 2689 output tokens allowed under a $0.0300 cap, cost $0.0289, truncated at the allowance. Board handles are shown here; the judge saw the answers with them removed._

**Recommendation.** Run a strangler-style migration in which the monolith stays the system of record for credentials until the very end, and the provider takes over in stages behind a feature flag. Use the provider's login-time credential hook (lazy migration) for everyone who logs in during the quarter, then run a planned bulk import of the remaining dormant users' bcrypt hashes near the end if the provider supports hash import, with one-time emailed sign-in links as the fallback. There is exactly one step that is hard to undo in under an hour (deleting the legacy password hashes and login code), and that step is deferred until after the quarter's cutover has run cleanly for several weeks. Everything before it is a flag flip or a routing change you can revert in minutes.

**Design principles that make this reversible.** First, the monolith keeps validating its own sessions and keeps its password table untouched throughout; the provider only ever receives copies. Second, every behavioural change ships behind a flag that is evaluated per user or per percentage, so rollback is a config change, not a deploy. Third, the application's notion of identity is decoupled from the login mechanism early: a stable internal user ID is mapped to the provider's subject identifier (the OIDC `sub` claim) in a link table, so no downstream code depends on which system authenticated the user. Fourth, the hook calls back into the monolith over an authenticated endpoint that verifies the bcrypt hash, so the hashes never leave your database during the lazy phase and the monolith remains the single authority until cutover.

**Step 1 (weeks 1-2): Foundations, no user-visible change.** Stand up the provider tenant in staging and production. Configure the OIDC application, redirect URIs, token lifetimes and the custom domain. Add the identity link table (internal user ID, provider `sub`, migrated-at timestamp, migration method). Build a thin authentication abstraction in the monolith with two implementations, legacy and OIDC, selected by a flag defaulted to legacy. Instrument login success and failure rates, latency and error codes now, so you have a baseline to compare against later. Verify that usernames map cleanly to the provider's identifier model, and decide which attribute is the unique key (immutable internal ID, not username or email, since those can change). At the end of this step, production behaviour is identical to today, the flag exists, and you have a dashboard baseline. Rollback is trivial because nothing is live.

**Step 2 (weeks 3-4): Build and test the migration hook against staging with synthetic and cloned data.** Implement the credential hook: when a user submits a password to the provider and no local credential exists, the provider calls your verification endpoint with the username and password; the endpoint looks up the bcrypt hash, verifies it, applies your existing lockout and rate limiting, and returns a profile on success. The provider then stores the credential in its own store and marks the user migrated, so subsequent logins never touch the monolith. Secure the endpoint with mutual TLS or a signed shared secret plus IP allow-listing, never log passwords, and make it constant-time with respect to unknown users to avoid enumeration. Load-test it at several times your peak login rate. Also decide how the bcrypt cost factor is handled: users verified through the hook are re-hashed by the provider under its own scheme, so you do not need to preserve your cost parameters. At the end, the hook is proven in staging; production is unchanged.

**Step 3 (weeks 5-6): Shadow mode in production.** Without sending any user to the provider for login, start creating provider-side user records for new signups through a dual write (the monolith remains authoritative and the provider copy is created without credentials or with a placeholder). Run the OIDC login path internally for staff accounts only, via the flag scoped to an employee allow-list. This exercises the real redirect flow, token validation and session creation in production with people who can tolerate a failure. At the end, staff log in through the provider via the hook, the link table is being populated, and customers still use the legacy path exclusively. Rollback is removing the staff scoping from the flag, which takes seconds.

**Step 4 (weeks 7-9): Progressive rollout to customers.** Move the flag to 1%, then 5%, 25%, 50% of users, bucketing by a stable hash of the internal user ID so a given user does not flip between systems from request to request. Hold at each stage for several days and compare login success rate, support tickets and latency against the Step 1 baseline, with explicit abort thresholds agreed in advance (for example, a login failure rate more than a fixed margin above baseline). Dormant users are untouched at this point; each active user migrates on their first login in the new path. At the end of this step, roughly half of active users have credentials in the provider, the rest are still served by legacy, and both systems answer authentication correctly. Rollback is dropping the percentage to zero: users already migrated can still log in on the legacy path because their monolith hash was never modified.

**The consequence of that last point is a rule: during the dual-running period, password changes must be written to both places.** If a migrated user changes their password at the provider, the monolith hash goes stale, and a rollback would lock them out with their new password. Two options: route all password changes through the provider and have it call a webhook that updates the monolith's bcrypt hash (you can accept the plaintext only inside the provider's hook, if it exposes one, and otherwise disable password change in the new path until after cutover), or simply freeze password changes for flagged users during the dual period and tell them why. The freeze is simpler and, with a few weeks of duration, acceptable. Choose it unless the provider offers a reliable post-change hook.

**Step 5 (weeks 10-11): 100% rollout and the dormant-user bulk migration.** Move the flag to 100%. Everyone who logs in now migrates automatically. For users who have not logged in, you have two sequenced tactics. If the provider supports bulk import of bcrypt hashes (most major ones do, as users with a password hash in a documented format), import the remaining hashes in batches off-peak, verifying counts and spot-checking logins with test accounts; the provider verifies bcrypt natively and can rehash on first successful login. This is the preferred path because those users then experience nothing at all. If the provider cannot import bcrypt, do not email a mass reset; instead leave the legacy hook in place for as long as needed and let dormant users migrate whenever they return, accepting that the legacy hash table must be retained for them. For accounts that are plausibly abandoned, decide a retention policy with legal and product: for example, after a defined period of inactivity, those accounts are archived rather than migrated, and returning users go through a normal recovery flow, which is a policy decision you make explicitly rather than a forced reset imposed on active people. At the end, every active user can log in at the provider, either already migrated or importable, and the hook is still available as a safety net.

**Sessions while both systems are live.** The monolith's server-side session store remains the only session the application trusts. A successful OIDC login produces an authorization code exchange; the monolith validates the ID token (signature, issuer, audience, nonce, expiry), maps `sub` to the internal user through the link table, and then creates its normal server-side session exactly as legacy login does. Downstream code therefore sees one kind of session regardless of how the user authenticated. Existing sessions are never invalidated by the rollout: users logged in before their bucket flips keep their session until it expires naturally, and nobody is bounced mid-task. Logout should destroy the local session and, for OIDC users, also call the provider's end-session endpoint so single sign-out is consistent; for rollback safety, local logout must work even if the provider is unreachable. Keep session lifetimes unchanged during the transition so behaviour is predictable. Support both cookie names or a single cookie with a source attribute only if you must, but prefer one session format. If you later want the provider's tokens to drive sessions, that is a separate follow-up, not part of this migration.
