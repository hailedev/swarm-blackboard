# A six-week teen reading programme on a $500 budget

Chosen as the baseline's widest win. Problem `teen-reading-programme`.

## Problem

A public library branch in a mid-sized town wants to run a six-week programme for 13-to-17-year-olds, most of whom do not use the library today. One librarian can give four hours a week; volunteers can be recruited; the budget is $500 for everything beyond the room and the existing collection. The branch manager will repeat the programme only if there is evidence it changed something for the participants, not just that some turned up.

Design the programme: what happens each week and why teenagers who do not read for pleasure would come, how the library reaches teens who are not already visitors, how the $500 is spent, and how the outcome is measured within the six weeks in a way that goes beyond attendance. Make sure it fits four hours of librarian time a week.

### Constraints

- A public library branch in a mid-sized town; one librarian can give four hours a week, plus volunteers if recruited.
- Budget is $500 in total for the six weeks; the library already has a meeting room and a book collection.
- The target is 13-to-17-year-olds, most of whom do not currently use the library.
- The branch manager wants evidence of the outcome, not only attendance numbers, to decide whether to repeat it.

### Success criteria

- A programme design (format, frequency, content) that a non-reading teenager would plausibly turn up to.
- A recruitment plan that reaches teens who do not use the library today.
- A budget that allocates the $500 and shows what it buys.
- Outcome measures beyond attendance, measurable within six weeks, and how they are collected.

Recommendation: run a six-week maker-based programme for 13-to-17-year-olds (a 'Creator's Lab') in which each week ends with a tangible object, recruited through peer referral and visible presence at youth hubs, with the $500 split as $100 for twenty $5 gift cards and $400 for materials, snacks and volunteer background checks. Outcomes are measured by a short anonymous pre/post self-efficacy survey plus a rubric score of each weekly artefact. Confidence is low: this is a conditional recommendation that still needs a costed plan, a named librarian and named volunteers before launch.

Format and content: weeks 1-2 are short hands-on making sessions (for example a card or board-game prototype and a short audio intro recorded on a phone) so a non-reader leaves with a visible object; weeks 3-4 extend the build using spoken or visual rather than written content; weeks 5-6 finish a shared product shown to families or peers in week 6. Sessions run once a week for about two hours, within the librarian's four hours, with the remaining time used for recruitment contact and rubric scoring. Volunteers run logistics and the anonymous digital survey so the librarian is not the sole data collector.

Recruitment: the aim is to reach teens who do not use the library, which peer referral alone does not guarantee, since the referral evidence concerns existing attendees. The plan therefore needs visible presence at non-library youth spaces such as schools, sports clubs or cafes, but no hub, staffing time or permission has yet been named, so this channel is unverified and must be confirmed before launch.

Budget: $100 buys twenty $5 gift cards for completion incentives; $400 covers craft and making materials, snacks and volunteer background checks. The board does not yet itemise the $400 or show that background checks at roughly $20-$50 per volunteer fit within it, and the incentive rules (per session, per attendance or per survey) are not settled, so the allocation is provisional.

Measurement: a two-question anonymous digital self-efficacy survey at week 1 and week 6, administered by a volunteer on a tablet, plus a rubric score of each weekly artefact. Both are self-report or single-rater measures and are open to social desirability and rater inconsistency; the tablets' source and cost, the survey tool and who scores the rubric are not yet specified. Results will show whether participants changed in their own reported confidence and in what they produced, not whether the change lasts.

Open questions before launch: who the named librarian and volunteers are; how the four-hour cap holds once tablet collection, briefing and rubric scoring are counted; whether recruitment reaches non-users in practice; and whether the evidence cited for making-based and peer-referral approaches applies to teens who do not already use libraries. The branch manager should treat the six-week result as a signal of change, not proof, and decide on repetition only once these gaps are closed.

## Baseline answer

_claude-sonnet-5-5, asked for about 1350 words, used 2696 of 2696 output tokens allowed under a $0.0300 cap, cost $0.0290, truncated at the allowance. Board handles are shown here; the judge saw the answers with them removed._

**Recommendation.** Run a six-week, one-evening-a-week "maker and media studio" for teens, not a book club. Each week ends in something the teen makes and keeps or shares: a zine, a podcast episode, a short stop-motion film, a game, a playlist with liner notes. Books and library resources are used as raw material (research, sound effects, source stories, reference), so reading happens as a by-product rather than as the pitch. Measure change with a short before-and-after check-in on three things the library can plausibly move in six weeks: how teens use the library, how they feel about it, and what they do with it afterward. Recruit through places teens already are, using teen ambassadors and a school partner, not posters inside the library. The librarian's four hours a week are protected by recruiting two to three adult volunteers to run the sessions with her and by keeping prep to a fixed template.

**Why non-readers would come.** Teens who do not read for pleasure are rarely hostile to the library; they have no reason to think it has anything for them. They show up for three things: something to make, people their age, and some say over what happens. So the programme is built around those. Sessions run 90 minutes after school (4:00 to 5:30 on one weekday, chosen after asking the partner school what clashes with sport and buses), with food at the start. Teens choose the output track in week one by vote, and each week has a visible product so nobody is told to "come and discover reading." The library is the venue and the supplier, not the subject.

**The six weeks.** Week 1, "Pitch it": food, a quick icebreaker, a 15-minute demo of what is possible (a two-minute zine page, a phone-recorded audio clip, a stop-motion frame), then teens form small groups, pick a project from three tracks (zine/comic, audio/podcast, stop-motion/short film), and write a one-line pitch. Teens also do the baseline check-in (described below) and get a library card issued on the spot if they want one, with no fines on teen cards for the duration. Week 2, "Find your material": each group does a guided hunt in the collection and online databases for something their project needs: a true story, a visual reference, a music or sound idea, a fact that makes their piece better. The librarian or a volunteer acts as a researcher-for-hire, which reframes the library as a service teens can use rather than a place with rules. Teens leave with a stack of two or three items they chose for their own reasons, which is the first real borrowing event.

Week 3, "Make the first draft": a pure workshop session. Volunteers (a local artist, a student from the community college media course, a retired teacher or radio hobbyist) coach technique. The librarian circulates. Week 4, "Make it better": peer feedback in a structured format (one thing that works, one question, one suggestion), then revision. This is the week where attrition usually bites, so a text reminder goes out the day before with a photo of the group's work in progress. Week 5, "Finish and frame": completion, titling, writing a 50-word creator note, and deciding how to share. Week 6, "Showcase": an open evening in the meeting room for friends, family and school staff, with pieces displayed or screened, a printed zine anthology, and a QR code to the audio and video work on a shared page. Teens choose whether work is credited by name or pseudonym, and parental permission covers any public sharing. The showcase doubles as the library's evidence-gathering moment and its best recruitment event for the next cohort.

**Fitting four hours a week.** The librarian's time is the binding constraint, so the design assigns it deliberately. Ninety minutes of session time plus 30 minutes of setup and teardown is two hours. The remaining two hours cover recruitment and communications in weeks 1 to 2 (shifting to prep and follow-up later), buying and preparing materials, and the evaluation work. Volunteers carry the technical coaching, the food run and the sign-in. A volunteer coordinator role is given to one reliable adult (a Friends of the Library member, for example) who handles scheduling and reminders, so the librarian is not the only point of contact. Before week 1, the librarian will need roughly four to six hours of one-off setup, which should be drawn from the four-hour weeks of the two recruitment weeks before launch (a two-week pre-launch period is worth building in; it is outside the six weeks but inside normal workload planning). Volunteers need a basic background check per library policy, and teens should never be alone with a single adult; two adults are present at every session.

**Reaching teens who do not use the library.** Do not rely on in-branch signage; the target group by definition does not see it. Four channels, in order of expected yield. First, a school partnership: ask the principal or a librarian, counsellor or head of year at the nearest secondary school to let the library staff do a five-minute announcement at an assembly or lunch, and to include a flyer or link in the school's regular communications. Second, teen ambassadors: recruit three to five teens (include a couple who already use the library, and ask them to bring a friend who does not) with a small incentive such as a credential for school service hours or community-service records, and have them post to their own networks and personally invite peers. Peer invitation consistently outperforms adult-issued flyers. Third, places teens already congregate: youth sports clubs, skate park, community centre, a games or comics shop, the local fast-food or bubble-tea venue where a flyer or counter card can be left with permission. Fourth, social media in a low-effort form: two or three short posts on the library's existing accounts and on a community Facebook or Instagram page, showing the actual products (a sample zine page, a 10-second film clip) rather than the words "teen programme." Frame every message around the output ("Make a zine / podcast / short film. Free. Food. Fridays 4pm") and not the venue. Registration is a short form or a text to a number, with a parent consent step built in for under-16s. Aim for 12 to 15 registrations to get 8 to 10 regular attenders; cap at 16 so the room and the volunteers can cope.

**The $500.** Food and drink for six sessions, about $120 (roughly $20 per session for 12 to 15 teens: pizza slices, fruit, water). Materials and small equipment, about $150: art and zine supplies, paper and a stapler, a couple of cheap USB microphones or lapel mics, a tripod or two for phone filming, a set of modelling clay and cardboard for stop-motion; free software (Audacity, a free stop-motion app, Canva's free tier) means no licences. Printing the zine anthology and showcase displays, about $50. Recruitment, about $60: flyers and counter cards, plus small thank-you gifts for teen ambassadors, such as $10 gift cards. Volunteer thank-yous and background-check costs if not covered by the library, about $50. Participant incentives, about $50: a small prize or certificate for finishing and, where permitted, a $5 or $10 gift card drawn at random for completing the final survey (this lifts response rates, which matters for the evidence). Contingency, about $20. The total is $500. If the library already owns phones, tablets or a projector, the equipment line shrinks and the difference goes to food or a second showcase print run. No money is spent on guest facilitators; volunteers cover that, and local artists or students will often give two hours for a credit line and a meal.
