# A six-week teen reading programme on a $500 budget

Chosen as the swarm's widest win. Problem `teen-reading-programme`.

## Problem

A public library branch in a mid-sized town wants to run a six-week programme for 13-to-17-year-olds, most of whom do not use the library today. One librarian can give four hours a week; volunteers can be recruited; the budget is $500 for everything beyond the room and the existing collection. The branch manager will repeat the programme only if there is evidence it changed something for the participants, not just that some turned up.

Design the programme: what happens each week and why teenagers who do not read for pleasure would come, how the library reaches teens who are not already visitors, how the $500 is spent, and how the outcome is measured within the six weeks in a way that goes beyond attendance. Make sure it fits four hours of librarian time a week.

### Constraints

- A public library branch in a mid-sized town; one librarian can give four hours a week, plus volunteers if recruited.
- Budget is $500 in total for the six weeks; the library already has a meeting room and a book collection.
- The target is 13-to-17-year-olds, most of whom do not currently use the library.
- The branch manager wants evidence of the outcome, not only attendance numbers, to decide whether to repeat it.

### Success criteria

- A programme design (format, frequency, content) that a non-reading teenager would plausibly turn up to.
- A recruitment plan that reaches teens who do not use the library today.
- A budget that allocates the $500 and shows what it buys.
- Outcome measures beyond attendance, measurable within six weeks, and how they are collected.

The Library Game Lab is a six-week interactive programme designed to attract teenagers aged 13 to 17 who do not currently use the library by offering a low-barrier, high-engagement activity centered on analog game design. Instead of traditional reading programs, participants will spend their time building physical card and board games, creating tangible artifacts that provide immediate satisfaction and social value. This format leverages the natural interest adolescents have in gaming and competition while introducing library resources through a creative lens. The programme runs for one hour each week, fitting within a four-hour weekly librarian constraint when combined with volunteer support for setup and teardown.

To reach teens who are not already visitors, the recruitment strategy relies entirely on a Peer Ambassador model, avoiding paid advertising to keep costs near zero. Three teen ambassadors from local high schools will be recruited to spread the word through existing social networks, class announcements, and school messaging apps. Ambassadors receive free snacks during sessions and a certificate of recognition for their efforts, leveraging peer trust to normalize library attendance. To further extend reach, short digital teasers featuring student-made games will be shared on local community social media groups, using images of the physical prototypes created in previous weeks to demonstrate credibility without requiring a marketing budget.

The six-week agenda is structured to maintain engagement and prevent the typical drop-off seen in mid-programme activities.
