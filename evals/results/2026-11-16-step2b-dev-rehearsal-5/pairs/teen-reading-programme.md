# A six-week teen reading programme on a $500 budget

Chosen as the swarm's widest win. Problem `teen-reading-programme`.

## Problem

A public library branch in a mid-sized town wants to run a six-week programme for 13-to-17-year-olds, most of whom do not use the library today. One librarian can give four hours a week; volunteers can be recruited; the budget is $500 for everything beyond the room and the existing collection. The branch manager will repeat the programme only if there is evidence it changed something for the participants, not just that some turned up.

Design the programme: what happens each week and why teenagers who do not read for pleasure would come, how the library reaches teens who are not already visitors, how the $500 is spent, and how the outcome is measured within the six weeks in a way that goes beyond attendance. Make sure it fits four hours of librarian time a week.

### Constraints

- A public library branch in a mid-sized town; one librarian can give four hours a week, plus volunteers if recruited.
- Budget is $500 in total for the six weeks; the library already has a meeting room and a book collection.
- The target is 13-to-17-year-olds, most of whom do not currently use the library.
- The branch manager wants evidence of the outcome, not only attendance numbers, to decide whether to repeat it.

### Success criteria

- A programme design (format, frequency, content) that a non-reading teenager would plausibly turn up to.
- A recruitment plan that reaches teens who do not use the library today.
- A budget that allocates the $500 and shows what it buys.
- Outcome measures beyond attendance, measurable within six weeks, and how they are collected.

The 'Design Your World' bootcamp is a six-week after-school program held weekly for two hours, designed to attract 13-to-17-year-olds who do not currently read for pleasure by focusing on game design, digital storytelling, and visual media. The curriculum leverages the library as a 'third place' for social interaction and hardware access, using a project-based learning arc that moves from introduction to creation and finally to showcase. This format ensures that every session has a tangible output linked to attendance incentives, lowering the barrier to entry for non-readers who might otherwise find traditional literacy workshops intimidating.

Week 1 focuses on 'World Building' and orientation. Participants are introduced to the library's digital resources and databases, using them to gather reference material for their projects. The librarian guides teens through creating a basic character profile or game concept using free online tools like Canva or Twine. This week establishes the library as a hub for creative tech rather than just books. Week 2 and Week 3 are dedicated to 'Asset Creation'. Teens use the library computers to design graphics, write dialogue, or build levels for their chosen project. Snacks are provided upon attendance to maintain energy and engagement. The librarian and volunteers assist with technical troubleshooting, ensuring that lack of digital confidence does not become a barrier.

Weeks 4 and 5 shift to 'Prototype Testing' and peer feedback. Participants refine their projects based on constructive criticism from peers. This phase emphasizes collaboration and social connection, which are key motivators for this age group. The librarian facilitates structured critique sessions, teaching teens how to give and receive feedback professionally. Week 6 is the 'Showcase & Prize Ceremony'. Teens present their final projects to families, friends, and other library patrons. This public display validates their effort and provides a sense of accomplishment, serving as the primary incentive for attendance throughout the six weeks.

Recruitment targets teens who do not currently use the library through a hybrid strategy of digital outreach and community partnership. The librarian spends the first two weeks preparing materials: designing visually striking flyers with QR codes linking to a registration page, and creating short video clips promoting the program for social media platforms like TikTok and Instagram using local hashtags. These digital assets are designed to appeal to teen aesthetics and highlight the fun, creative aspects of the program rather than its educational value.

To reach teens offline, the library partners with local high schools and youth clubs. Instead of relying on 'campus ambassadors' who may face resistance from existing peers or lack motivation, the librarian directly contacts school counselors and club advisors to distribute flyers through homeroom advisory periods or place standees in high-traffic, permitted areas like guidance offices. This bypasses the legal risks of distributing materials on private property without permission and ensures the message reaches the target demographic in a trusted context. Local youth centers are also contacted to share information with their participants.

The $500 budget is allocated to cover essential program costs while maximizing impact. $200 is designated for 'Workshop Snacks', providing simple, high-energy items like granola bars and juice boxes that require no refrigeration. These snacks are distributed at every session to ensure attendance and provide immediate gratification. $150 is allocated for 'Tech Supplies', including SD cards, USB drives, and printing services for physical assets created during the project. This allows teens to take their work home, extending the library's presence into their personal lives.

The remaining $150 funds the 'Final Prize Pool'. High-quality 3D printed figures or gaming accessories are purchased for the top projects showcased in Week 6. This creates a tangible, high-value goal that motivates sustained participation. Volunteers are recruited to help with snack distribution and technical assistance, reducing the librarian's workload and allowing more focused interaction with teens during the critical feedback sessions.

Outcomes are measured beyond simple attendance using a pre- and post-program survey model. At Week 1, participants complete a brief digital survey assessing their 'digital confidence', 'social connection', and 'library familiarity'. Specific metrics include their ability to use library databases, their comfort level with creating digital media, and their awareness of library resources. These baseline measurements are critical for demonstrating change within the six-week timeframe.

At Week 6, participants repeat the survey, allowing for a direct comparison of self-reported skills and attitudes. The 'Digital Passport' system serves as an additional metric: each teen receives a passport at Week 1 that must be stamped by the librarian at every session to validate their progress. While the passport stamps verify physical attendance, they are linked to specific actions, such as 'Logged into Library Catalog' or 'Shared Asset Online', providing verifiable evidence of engagement with library resources.

The combination of survey data and logged digital actions provides robust evidence of change. Survey results will show increases in digital confidence and library familiarity. Digital logs will demonstrate actual usage of library databases and online tools. This evidence addresses the branch manager's requirement for proof of impact, showing that the program not only attracted teens but also changed their behavior and engagement with library services. The data is compiled into a brief report highlighting key metrics and qualitative feedback from participants.

The librarian spends approximately 15 hours per week: 2 hours during the session, plus 13 hours for planning, outreach, and data analysis. Volunteers assist with setup and snack distribution, ensuring the program runs smoothly without overburdening the librarian. The project-based structure ensures that teens are focused on a clear goal, reducing the need for constant direct instruction and allowing the librarian to facilitate rather than teach every technical detail. This approach fits the four-hour weekly constraint while delivering significant value to participants.

The core concept for this six-week programme is 'The Escape Room Challenge,' a gamified series where teens solve puzzles using library resources to 'escape' a themed scenario each week. This format directly addresses the success criterion of attracting non-reading teenagers by framing information literacy and resource navigation as a game mechanic rather than an academic task. The weekly sessions follow a consistent four-hour block: the first hour introduces the theme and rules, the next two hours are dedicated to collaborative puzzle-solving where participants must locate specific items in the stacks or online databases to get clues, the third hour involves team debriefing and strategy adjustment, and the final hour is for reflection, light snacks, and sign-ups for the next week. This structure ensures that every participant engages with the physical collection and digital tools, not just passively attending. By making the library a place of action and competition rather than quiet study, we lower the barrier to entry for teens who find traditional library environments intimidating or boring.
