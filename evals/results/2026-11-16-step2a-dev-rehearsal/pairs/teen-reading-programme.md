# A six-week teen reading programme on a $500 budget

Chosen as the swarm's widest win. Problem `teen-reading-programme`.

## Problem

A public library branch in a mid-sized town wants to run a six-week programme for 13-to-17-year-olds, most of whom do not use the library today. One librarian can give four hours a week; volunteers can be recruited; the budget is $500 for everything beyond the room and the existing collection. The branch manager will repeat the programme only if there is evidence it changed something for the participants, not just that some turned up.

Design the programme: what happens each week and why teenagers who do not read for pleasure would come, how the library reaches teens who are not already visitors, how the $500 is spent, and how the outcome is measured within the six weeks in a way that goes beyond attendance. Make sure it fits four hours of librarian time a week.

### Constraints

- A public library branch in a mid-sized town; one librarian can give four hours a week, plus volunteers if recruited.
- Budget is $500 in total for the six weeks; the library already has a meeting room and a book collection.
- The target is 13-to-17-year-olds, most of whom do not currently use the library.
- The branch manager wants evidence of the outcome, not only attendance numbers, to decide whether to repeat it.

### Success criteria

- A programme design (format, frequency, content) that a non-reading teenager would plausibly turn up to.
- A recruitment plan that reaches teens who do not use the library today.
- A budget that allocates the $500 and shows what it buys.
- Outcome measures beyond attendance, measurable within six weeks, and how they are collected.

Recommendation: Launch a six-week 'Game Design & Maker' Hybrid Series for teens aged 13–17, utilizing analog prototyping and narrative design as the primary hook. This approach bypasses high technical barriers and expensive hardware, focusing instead on storytelling, rule creation, and physical game construction using low-cost materials. The programme is designed to be led by one librarian with minimal volunteer support, fitting strictly within a four-hour weekly capacity and a $500 total budget.

Programme Design: The series is structured to shift from high-librarian input in the early weeks to peer-facilitated creation later, ensuring engagement without burning out the single staff member. Week 1 focuses on 'Hook & Identify,' where teens play existing library board games and design simple analog prototypes to build comfort. Week 2, 'Deconstruct,' involves analyzing game mechanics and drafting rules for their own creations. Week 3, 'Prototype,' is dedicated to building physical versions of their games using paper, cardstock, and dice. Week 4, 'Playtest,' allows teens to peer-test each other's designs in a structured feedback loop. Week 5, 'Polish & Record,' sees the finalization of game assets and the recording of short video trailers or audio explanations using library PCs. Week 6 concludes with a 'Showcase,' where participants present their games to peers and community members.

The content appeals to non-reading teenagers by treating games as creative artifacts rather than competitive products. By focusing on narrative and mechanics—using free tools like Twine for story logic or simple paper-and-pen methods—the programme avoids the skill barriers of complex coding or high-skill esports. This leverages existing library board games and cheap consumables, making the barrier to entry low and the tangible output (a playable game) highly desirable.

Recruitment Strategy: To reach teens who do not currently use the library, the programme relies on visual, peer-to-peer recruitment channels rather than formal institutional advertising. The strategy employs a 'Teens Create Teens' campaign, recruiting 2-3 influential local teens as 'Program Ambassadors' to share the event on their personal Instagram and TikTok stories. This leverages high-trust peer networks and bypasses filtered school channels or ineffective generic posters.

The recruitment plan is supported by $100 allocated for 'Guerrilla Digital' boosts, used to promote three high-impact posts featuring teen ambassadors and game design previews on local social media platforms. Additionally, 100 professional-looking 'Challenge Cards' are printed and distributed in informal youth spaces such as skate parks and community centers. These cards feature a QR code linking to the sign-up page, driving interest through curiosity and peer endorsement rather than traditional library branding.

Budget Allocation: The $500 budget is allocated to maximize tangible value and recruitment reach while adhering strictly to financial constraints. First, $150 is designated for 'Maker Kits,' purchasing high-quality paper, cardstock, dice, markers, and basic craft supplies required for analog game prototyping. This ensures every participant leaves with the materials to continue their creative work at home.

Second, $100 is allocated for 'Guerrilla Digital' costs, specifically for boosting recruitment posts on Instagram and TikTok to ensure visibility among non-library users who may not follow official branch accounts. Third, $150 is reserved for the final 'Game Jam' showcase incentives, providing small gift cards or library merchandise to reward participation and completion of the programme.

The remaining $100 is designated for essential operational costs, including volunteer support for background checks and liability waivers (critical for managing minors in off-hours activities), as well as a contingency fund for tech support and unexpected resource needs. This allocation ensures that the bulk of the budget directly serves the participants' creative output and the programme's visibility, with minimal overhead.

Outcome Measurement: To satisfy the branch manager’s requirement for evidence of change beyond simple attendance, the programme measures 'Behavioral Shifts' through hard data collected within the six-week window. The primary metric is the number of unique library card applications generated during the program, specifically tracked in Week 1 to capture initial interest and commitment from non-users.

Secondary metrics include a 'Skill Badge' system where teens earn digital or physical badges for completing specific design tasks, such as 'Created a Map' or 'Wrote 500 Words.' This provides auditable data on skill acquisition and engagement. Additionally, a 'Digital Portfolio' is maintained by tracking the number of game designs submitted via library PCs during the session, capturing behavioral engagement data while participants are physically present.

A pre-session digital survey (Likert scale) is administered in Week 1 to measure baseline 'Digital Confidence' and 'Perceived Agency,' with a post-session reflection question collected at the Week 6 showcase. This allows for a comparison of self-efficacy before and after the programme, providing qualitative evidence of impact alongside the quantitative data.

The collection method is designed to fit within the four-hour weekly window: library staff track card applications and badge completions in real-time during sessions, while the pre/post surveys are administered digitally via short forms on library PCs, requiring only five minutes of librarian time per participant. This ensures that data collection is seamless and does not add significant administrative burden.

Assumption: The recruitment strategy assumes that 2-3 local teen influencers can be successfully recruited and authorized to share library content without formal school partnership restrictions. If this fails, the $100 digital boost will be redirected to targeted local Facebook group ads, which remain effective for mid-sized town outreach.

Assumption: The outcome measurement assumes that non-library-using teens aged 13-17 can apply for library cards with minimal parental friction. If parental consent creates a barrier, the 'Skill Badge' and 'Digital Portfolio' metrics will serve as primary proxies for engagement, with card applications tracked as a secondary success indicator.

Assumption: The 'Maker Kits' budget covers all necessary materials for analog prototyping. If supply costs fluctuate, the $100 contingency fund can be used to supplement craft supplies, ensuring that no participant is left without resources for their final showcase.

The core concept for this six-week programme is 'The Escape Room Challenge,' a gamified series where teens solve puzzles using library resources to 'escape' a themed scenario each week. This format directly addresses the success criterion of attracting non-reading teenagers by framing information literacy and resource navigation as a game mechanic rather than an academic task. The weekly sessions follow a consistent four-hour block: the first hour introduces the theme and rules, the next two hours are dedicated to collaborative puzzle-solving where participants must locate specific items in the stacks or online databases to get clues, the third hour involves team debriefing and strategy adjustment, and the final hour is for reflection, light snacks, and sign-ups for the next week. This structure ensures that every participant engages with the physical collection and digital tools, not just passively attending. By making the library a place of action and competition rather than quiet study, we lower the barrier to entry for teens who find traditional library environments intimidating or boring.
