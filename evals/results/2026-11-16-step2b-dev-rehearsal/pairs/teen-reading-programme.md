# A six-week teen reading programme on a $500 budget

Chosen as the swarm's widest win. Problem `teen-reading-programme`.

## Problem

A public library branch in a mid-sized town wants to run a six-week programme for 13-to-17-year-olds, most of whom do not use the library today. One librarian can give four hours a week; volunteers can be recruited; the budget is $500 for everything beyond the room and the existing collection. The branch manager will repeat the programme only if there is evidence it changed something for the participants, not just that some turned up.

Design the programme: what happens each week and why teenagers who do not read for pleasure would come, how the library reaches teens who are not already visitors, how the $500 is spent, and how the outcome is measured within the six weeks in a way that goes beyond attendance. Make sure it fits four hours of librarian time a week.

### Constraints

- A public library branch in a mid-sized town; one librarian can give four hours a week, plus volunteers if recruited.
- Budget is $500 in total for the six weeks; the library already has a meeting room and a book collection.
- The target is 13-to-17-year-olds, most of whom do not currently use the library.
- The branch manager wants evidence of the outcome, not only attendance numbers, to decide whether to repeat it.

### Success criteria

- A programme design (format, frequency, content) that a non-reading teenager would plausibly turn up to.
- A recruitment plan that reaches teens who do not use the library today.
- A budget that allocates the $500 and shows what it buys.
- Outcome measures beyond attendance, measurable within six weeks, and how they are collected.

To ensure repeatability and demonstrate tangible impact to the branch manager, this plan proposes 'Teen Tech Lab,' a six-week workshop series designed specifically for 13-to-17-year-olds who do not currently use the library. The programme replaces traditional reading activities with high-engagement digital creation and maker challenges, leveraging the library as a tech hub rather than just a book depot. By combining free software tools with low-cost physical materials and strong peer recruitment strategies, the programme fits within a $500 budget and a four-hour weekly librarian commitment while providing clear evidence of participant growth.

The core activity rotates between three 'hooks' that require no prior reading pleasure: digital storytelling using free video editing tools, collaborative cardboard engineering (making), and audio podcasting. These activities are chosen because research indicates teens are more motivated by social media creation and hands-on making than by traditional library offerings. The curriculum is structured so that each week builds a tangible product—a 60-second video trailer, a functional cardboard device, or a short audio clip—providing immediate gratification and a shareable outcome.

Recruitment focuses on reaching teens who do not currently use the library through a 'Peer Influencer' model rather than institutional advertising. The librarian recruits five local teen influencers via TikTok duets and school club connections to co-design the first week's activities. These ambassadors receive 'Library VIP' status or small gift cards (funded from the budget) in exchange for promoting the program on their personal geo-targeted social accounts. Peer trust outweighs institutional branding, making this the most effective low-cost channel for reaching non-users.

To make the programme accessible and reduce barriers, the plan utilizes free educational tools like Canva for Education and Audacity for audio editing. These platforms are free for K-12 educators and institutions, eliminating software licensing costs. The physical components rely on recycled cardboard and basic supplies sourced from dollar stores or local delivery companies, keeping material costs under $20 per week. This 'maker' approach frames the library as a creative space, appealing to teens interested in gaming, music, or DIY crafts.

Recruitment is further supported by leveraging existing administrative partnerships with local high schools. Teachers and school librarians distribute flyers directly to students who feel disconnected from the branch, using zero-cost channels. This bypasses the need for expensive static ads and ensures the program reaches a targeted demographic of teens who are already within the community's catchment area but not yet library patrons.

The $500 budget is strictly allocated to maximize engagement without exceeding the limit. $300 is allocated for snacks, providing pizza or tacos for 10-15 participants across six weeks. Food incentives are the most effective low-cost driver for teen attendance and retention. $150 is allocated for materials, covering the cost of USB drives, basic electronics, cardboard supplies, and printing for digital assets. $50 is reserved for prizes, including small gift cards for weekly challenges and a final 'best of show' award to maintain motivation throughout the series.

Schedule design accommodates the one librarian's four-hour weekly limit by splitting the time into two 2-hour sessions or utilizing heavy volunteer support for setup. Volunteers handle logistics during the first half, allowing the librarian to focus on facilitation during the core activity blocks. The weekly flow includes a 30-minute welcome and check-in, a 90-minute core digital or maker activity, a 15-minute brief library integration (e.g., showing how to find resources related to their project), and a 45-minute portfolio review and exit.

Pre-registration is required for all sessions to manage food costs and prepare material kits in advance. This ensures the librarian can prepare digital files and physical supplies before teens arrive, preventing chaos from walk-ins. Pre-registration also allows for accurate headcounts, mitigating the risk of food waste and ensuring that every participant has a dedicated workspace and necessary tools.

Outcome measurement goes beyond attendance by requiring each teen to create one digital artifact per week, forming a 'Digital Portfolio.' This portfolio serves as tangible evidence of skill acquisition and engagement. The librarian captures these artifacts via library tablets or local USB drives, avoiding reliance on cloud connectivity that may be unstable. The accumulation and quality of these artifacts provide clear data on participant growth.

Pre- and post-program surveys are administered via QR codes to measure shifts in library perception and self-reported confidence in using library tools. These short, 5-question surveys are low-burden and fit easily into the exit flow. Additionally, 'Competition Wins' are tracked as hard data for engagement, providing quantitative evidence of participation levels.

The combination of high-interest hooks, peer-driven recruitment, and a structured budget ensures the programme is attractive to non-users and manageable for the library staff. The use of free software and recycled materials keeps costs low while maintaining high quality. Pre-registration and volunteer support ensure the librarian can facilitate effectively within the four-hour limit.

Specific weekly themes include: Week 1 'Intro to Digital Storytelling' using Canva; Week 2 'Cardboard Engineering Challenges'; Week 3 'Podcast Production Basics'; Week 4 'Video Editing & Effects'; Week 5 'Final Polish of Portfolio Pieces'; and Week 6 'Showcase & Celebration'. This progression builds skills gradually, ensuring that even teens with no prior experience can produce a final project.

The branch manager will receive evidence of change through the collected digital portfolios, survey results showing improved library perception, and the number of new library card applications generated by participants. The tangible nature of the outputs (videos, podcasts, built items) serves as proof of engagement that goes beyond simple attendance records.

Assumption: Both schools agree to host a sign-up table; if one declines, sign-up moves to the branch lobby with peer ambassadors distributing flyers. This assumption rests on the need for initial reach and would be adjusted if school access is denied by increasing local social media ad spend from the prize budget.

The core concept for this six-week programme is 'The Escape Room Challenge,' a gamified series where teens solve puzzles using library resources to 'escape' a themed scenario each week. This format directly addresses the success criterion of attracting non-reading teenagers by framing information literacy and resource navigation as a game mechanic rather than an academic task. The weekly sessions follow a consistent four-hour block: the first hour introduces the theme and rules, the next two hours are dedicated to collaborative puzzle-solving where participants must locate specific items in the stacks or online databases to get clues, the third hour involves team debriefing and strategy adjustment, and the final hour is for reflection, light snacks, and sign-ups for the next week. This structure ensures that every participant engages with the physical collection and digital tools, not just passively attending. By making the library a place of action and competition rather than quiet study, we lower the barrier to entry for teens who find traditional library environments intimidating or boring.
